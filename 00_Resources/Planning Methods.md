# Planning Methods

*Read when the user asks how to plan their week or day, wants to follow a productivity method, or is choosing a planning style. The plan-my-week and start-my-day skills read the style recorded in Core Memory.*

There are many productivity methods, and most of them say the same few things in different words. What helps is picking the one that fits how you work, and letting your AI apply it to the files you already keep. None of these needs new folders, new lists or new apps. A method here is a way of looking at the same tasks, not more structure to maintain. Maintaining a system is a common reason people give one up.

Your choice is saved in Core Memory in root MEMORY.md in exactly this form, so the skills can read it:

```
- [YYYY-MM-DD] Planning style: Big Three.
```

You can combine two, such as "Planning style: Big Three with time blocking." If nothing is recorded, the skills use Big Three. Change it whenever you like by saying "change my planning style".

## The menu

| Method | In one line | Suits you if |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Big Three (default) | Three outcomes for the week, and one main thing each day | You want something simple that works for most weeks |
| Time blocking | Every important task gets a slot on the calendar | Your days fill up with other people's requests, or you drift |
| Eat the frog | The hardest important task goes first each day | You put off the big thing until the day is gone |
| 1-3-5 | One big, three medium and five small tasks a day | Your work is many small tasks plus the odd big one |
| Eisenhower | Sort by urgent and important: do, schedule, hand off or drop | You feel buried and everything looks urgent |
| Pomodoro | Work in 25-minute sessions with 5-minute breaks | Starting is the hard part, or you get pulled away easily |
| Getting Things Done | Capture everything, decide the next action, review weekly | Your life has many moving parts; this workspace already follows it |

### Big Three

Each week, pick three outcomes that would make the week a good one, such as "proposal sent" rather than "work on proposal". Each day, pick the one task that moves one of them furthest. Everything else fits around it.

- **Week plan:** three focus outcomes, each with a time set aside for it.
- **Daily brief:** the top three, with the day's main thing first.

### Time blocking

Give each important task a block of time on the calendar, as if it were a meeting with yourself. Group small tasks, such as email and errands, into one block. When something new comes in, it takes the place of a block rather than piling on top.

- **Week plan:** blocks for the focus outcomes and the tasks due this week, placed around fixed commitments.
- **Daily brief:** today's blocks in order, with what each is for.

### Eat the frog

Do the task you most want to avoid first, before email or messages. The rest of the day is easier once it is done.

- **Week plan:** one "frog" a day, named in advance, placed in the first block of the morning.
- **Daily brief:** the frog comes first, on its own line.

### 1-3-5

Each day, choose one big task, three medium ones and five small ones. Nine tasks is the ceiling, which stops the list from pretending the day is longer than it is.

- **Week plan:** the big task for each day, so the week's heavy work is spread out.
- **Daily brief:** the day as 1, 3 and 5.

### Eisenhower

Sort tasks by two questions: is it important, and is it urgent? Important and urgent: do it now. Important, not urgent: schedule it. Urgent, not important: hand it to someone else or do it quickly. Neither: drop it.

- **Week plan:** "important, not urgent" work gets blocks first, since that is the work that slips.
- **Daily brief:** tasks grouped by the four answers, with an offer to drop or hand off the bottom group.

### Pomodoro

Work for 25 minutes, then take a 5-minute break. After four sessions, take a longer break. The timer makes starting easier, and counting sessions shows how long things really take.

- **Week plan:** tasks sized in sessions, such as "draft report, 4 sessions".
- **Daily brief:** how many sessions the day's tasks need, against the time free.

### Getting Things Done

Capture everything, decide the next physical action for each item, keep those actions in lists, and review every week. This workspace is built on it: the inbox, Next Actions, projects and the weekly review are its parts. Choose it if you want the AI to be strict about it, such as turning a vague task into a clear next action every time.

- **Week plan:** follows the weekly review, and makes sure every project has a next action.
- **Daily brief:** next actions that fit today's time and place.

## What applies whatever method you pick

These come from research on how plans succeed and fail, and the plan-my-week skill applies them every time.

1. **Plans run long.** People reliably underestimate how long their own tasks take, which psychologists call the planning fallacy. Every block gets some slack, and part of each day stays unplanned for what turns up.
2. **When and where beats "this week".** A plan that says when and where you will do something, in an if-then form such as "on Tuesday after the school run, I draft the proposal at the kitchen table", is followed far more often than a goal alone. Each focus outcome gets one.
3. **Fixed commitments come first.** Meetings, appointments and travel go in before anything else, so the plan fits the real week.
4. **Look back before planning forward.** Comparing last week's plan with what actually happened is how estimates get better. It takes one minute, and it is the first step of each week's plan.
5. **The plan is a draft.** The AI proposes it, and you change it. Nothing goes on your calendar until you say yes.
